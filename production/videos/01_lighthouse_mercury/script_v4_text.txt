He floated a two-ton glass lens on a bathtub of liquid metal.
You see, the new lens was so heavy the old clockwork could barely turn it. The keeper cranked it by hand all night, and every time he stopped for a sandwich, a ship nearly hit the rocks.
So the company sends a young engineer. He doesn't even look at the gears. He pours a silver pool under it, gives it one push with his finger, and two tons of glass start spinning like a record.
The old keeper laughs. "Son, I've turned that thing by hand for thirty years. You can't float glass on a puddle."
But it works. Every night the beam sweeps perfectly, and every morning the keeper polishes that silver bath, humming, like it's his new best friend.
Then the humming stops.
First he forgets the supply boat's name. Then he forgets the captain. Then he forgets which one is the boat. His hands shake so hard he spills the lamp oil, twice. By winter he's yelling at the seagulls, convinced they're spying on him.
The whole town laughs. Thirty years alone on a rock finally cracked him.
But it wasn't the loneliness. You see, that silver bath was mercury. It lets heavy things spin with almost no friction, but slowly turns into invisible vapor. In that tiny sealed lamp room, he breathed it every single night.
The ships stayed safe. The man keeping them safe didn't.
